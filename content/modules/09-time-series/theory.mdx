## 1. Introduction to Temporal Data

### 1.1 The Nature of Time Series Data

Time series data represents observations collected sequentially over time, forming the backbone of countless real-world applications from financial markets to climate science, healthcare monitoring to industrial IoT.

**Formal Definition:**
A time series is a sequence of data points indexed in time order:

$$X = \{x_t : t \in T\}$$

where $T$ represents the time index set (discrete or continuous), and $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the observation at time $t$ (univariate when $d=1$, multivariate when $d>1$).

### 1.2 Key Characteristics of Temporal Data

| Characteristic | Description | Mathematical Representation |
|----------------|-------------|----------------------------|
| **Temporal Dependence** | Current values depend on past values | $P(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, ...)$ |
| **Non-IID Nature** | Violates independence assumption | $\text{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k}) \neq 0$ |
| **Trend** | Long-term directional movement | $T(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + ...$ |
| **Seasonality** | Periodic patterns | $S(t) = S(t + p)$ for period $p$ |
| **Cyclical Patterns** | Non-periodic oscillations | Irregular wave-like fluctuations |
| **Heteroscedasticity** | Changing variance over time | $\text{Var}(x_t) \neq \text{Var}(x_{t-k})$ |

### 1.3 Types of Time Series

**By Time Index:**
- **Discrete-time series:** Observations at regular intervals ($t \in \mathbb{Z}$)
- **Continuous-time series:** Observations at any time point ($t \in \mathbb{R}$)

**By Dimensionality:**
- **Univariate:** Single variable tracked over time $\{x_t\}$
- **Multivariate:** Multiple variables $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}$ where $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$

**By Stationarity:**
- **Stationary:** Statistical properties constant over time
- **Non-stationary:** Statistical properties evolve over time

### 1.4 Historical Context and Evolution

The study of time series dates back to ancient astronomy, but formal statistical methods emerged in the 20th century:

| Era | Key Developments | Pioneers |
|-----|-----------------|----------|
| 1920s-30s | Autoregressive models | Yule, Walker |
| 1970s | ARIMA framework | Box & Jenkins |
| 1980s-90s | ARCH/GARCH models | Engle, Bollerslev |
| 1990s-2000s | State space models, Kalman filtering | Harvey, Durbin |
| 2010s-present | Deep learning revolution | LSTM, TCN, Transformers |

## 2. Stationarity: Theory and Tests

### 2.1 The Stationarity Concept

Stationarity is a fundamental assumption in time series analysis. A stationary process has statistical properties that remain constant over time.

**Strict Stationarity:**
A time series $\{x_t\}$ is strictly stationary if for any $k$ and any set of time indices $t_1, t_2, ..., t_k$:

$$F(x_{t_1}, x_{t_2}, ..., x_{t_k}) = F(x_{t_1+h}, x_{t_2+h}, ..., x_{t_k+h})$$

for all time shifts $h$, where $F$ is the joint cumulative distribution function.

**Weak (Second-Order) Stationarity:**
A more practical definition requiring:
1. Constant mean: $E[x_t] = \mu$ for all $t$
2. Constant variance: $\text{Var}(x_t) = \sigma^2$ for all $t$
3. Autocovariance depends only on lag: $\gamma(k) = \text{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k})$

### 2.2 Why Stationarity Matters

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">

Most statistical models and many machine learning algorithms assume or benefit from stationarity: - Model parameters remain stable across time - Forecasts have consistent statistical properties - Standard errors and confidence intervals are valid - Cross-validation procedures become meaningful

</Callout>

### 2.3 Types of Non-Stationarity

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Trend Stationary** | Deterministic trend + stationary residuals | $x_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \epsilon_t$ |
| **Difference Stationary** | Requires differencing to achieve stationarity | Random walk: $x_t = x_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$ |
| **Structural Break** | Sudden change in statistical properties | Market crash, policy change |
| **Seasonal Non-stationarity** | Seasonal patterns change over time | Increasing seasonal amplitude |
| **Variance Non-stationarity** | Changing variance (heteroscedasticity) | Financial volatility clustering |

### 2.4 Stationarity Tests

#### 2.4.1 Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test

**Null Hypothesis:** The series has a unit root (non-stationary)

**Test Equation:**
$$\Delta x_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma x_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \delta_i \Delta x_{t-i} + \epsilon_t$$

Where:
- $\Delta x_t = x_t - x_{t-1}$ (first difference)
- $\gamma = 0$ under null hypothesis
- $p$ is the lag order (selected via information criteria)

**Decision Rule:**
- If test statistic &lt; critical value: Reject null (stationary)
- If test statistic > critical value: Fail to reject (non-stationary)

**Python Implementation:**
```python
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

result = adfuller(series, autolag='AIC')
print(f'ADF Statistic: {result[0]:.4f}')
print(f'p-value: {result[1]:.4f}')
print(f'Critical Values: {result[4]}')
```

#### 2.4.2 Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS) Test

**Null Hypothesis:** The series is stationary (opposite of ADF)

**Test Statistic:**
$$KPSS = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t^2}{T^2 \hat{\sigma}^2}$$

where $S_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} \hat{\epsilon}_i$ is the partial sum of residuals.

**Key Difference from ADF:**
- KPSS assumes stationarity under null
- Use both tests together for robust conclusions

| ADF Result | KPSS Result | Interpretation |
|------------|-------------|----------------|
| Stationary | Stationary | Series is stationary |
| Stationary | Non-stationary | Trend stationary, consider detrending |
| Non-stationary | Stationary | Difference stationary, apply differencing |
| Non-stationary | Non-stationary | Inconclusive, further investigation needed |

#### 2.4.3 Phillips-Perron (PP) Test

A non-parametric alternative to ADF that is robust to heteroscedasticity:

$$PP = ADF + \text{correction terms for serial correlation and heteroscedasticity}$$

#### 2.4.4 Zivot-Andrews Test

Tests for unit root allowing for a single structural break:

$$\Delta x_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma x_{t-1} + \delta DU_t + \theta DT_t + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i \Delta x_{t-i} + \epsilon_t$$

Where $DU_t$ and $DT_t$ are dummy variables for level and trend breaks.

### 2.5 Transformations to Achieve Stationarity

#### 2.5.1 Differencing

**First-order differencing:**
$$\nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} = (1 - B)x_t$$

Where $B$ is the backshift operator: $Bx_t = x_{t-1}$

**Second-order differencing:**
$$\nabla^2 x_t = (1 - B)^2 x_t = x_t - 2x_{t-1} + x_{t-2}$$

**Seasonal differencing:**
$$\nabla_s x_t = x_t - x_{t-s} = (1 - B^s)x_t$$

Where $s$ is the seasonal period.

**Guidelines for Differencing:**
- Apply differencing until ADF test indicates stationarity
- Over-differencing can introduce artificial patterns
- Use ACF to check if further differencing is needed

#### 2.5.2 Logarithmic Transformation

For series with increasing variance:
$$y_t = \log(x_t)$$

Properties:
- Stabilizes variance (variance proportional to level)
- Converts multiplicative relationships to additive
- Cannot be applied to zero or negative values

#### 2.5.3 Box-Cox Transformation

General power transformation:

$$y_t^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases}
\frac{x_t^\lambda - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \neq 0 \\
\log(x_t) & \lambda = 0
\end{cases}$$

The parameter $\lambda$ is estimated to maximize normality or stabilize variance.

#### 2.5.4 Power Transformations

**Square root transformation:**
$$y_t = \sqrt{x_t}$$

**Reciprocal transformation:**
$$y_t = \frac{1}{x_t}$$

### 2.6 Common Pitfalls in Stationarity Testing

**Pitfall 1: Ignoring Structural Breaks**
- Standard tests have low power when structural breaks exist
- Use tests that account for breaks (Zivot-Andrews, Lee-Strazicich)

**Pitfall 2: Over-differencing**
- Differencing a stationary series creates MA structure
- Check ACF of differenced series for over-differencing signs

**Pitfall 3: Seasonal Confusion**
- Non-seasonal tests may miss seasonal non-stationarity
- Apply seasonal unit root tests (Hegy, Canova-Hansen)

**Pitfall 4: Sample Size Issues**
- Small samples lead to low test power
- Large samples may reject null for trivial deviations

## 3. Autocorrelation and Partial Autocorrelation

### 3.1 Autocorrelation Function (ACF)

The autocorrelation function measures the linear dependence between observations at different time lags.

**Population Autocorrelation:**
$$\rho(k) = \frac{\text{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k})}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(x_t)\text{Var}(x_{t-k})}} = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)}$$

**Sample Autocorrelation:**
$$\hat{\rho}(k) = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(x_t - \bar{x})(x_{t-k} - \bar{x})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \bar{x})^2}$$

### 3.2 Properties of ACF

- **Symmetry:** $\rho(k) = \rho(-k)$
- **Bound:** $-1 \leq \rho(k) \leq 1$
- **At lag 0:** $\rho(0) = 1$
- **For white noise:** $\rho(k) = 0$ for $k \neq 0$

### 3.3 Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

PACF measures the correlation between $x_t$ and $x_{t-k}$ after removing the effect of intermediate lags $x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-k+1}$.

**Definition:**
$$\phi_{kk} = \text{Corr}(x_t, x_{t-k} | x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-k+1})$$

**Computation via Yule-Walker Equations:**

$$\begin{pmatrix}
\rho(0) & \rho(1) & \cdots & \rho(k-1) \\
\rho(1) & \rho(0) & \cdots & \rho(k-2) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\rho(k-1) & \rho(k-2) & \cdots & \rho(0)
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\phi_{k1} \\
\phi_{k2} \\
\vdots \\
\phi_{kk}
\end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\rho(1) \\
\rho(2) \\
\vdots \\
\rho(k)
\end{pmatrix}$$

### 3.4 ACF/PACF Patterns for Common Models

| Model | ACF Pattern | PACF Pattern |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| **AR(p)** | Tails off gradually | Cuts off after lag p |
| **MA(q)** | Cuts off after lag q | Tails off gradually |
| **ARMA(p,q)** | Tails off gradually | Tails off gradually |
| **Random Walk** | Very slow decay | $\phi_{11} \approx 1$, others near 0 |
| **Seasonal AR** | Decay at seasonal lags | Spikes at seasonal lags |

### 3.5 Confidence Intervals and Significance Testing

**Bartlett's Formula:**
For white noise, the standard error of $\hat{\rho}(k)$ is approximately:

$$\text{SE}[\hat{\rho}(k)] \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}$$

**95% Confidence Interval:**
$$\hat{\rho}(k) \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{T}}$$

**Ljung-Box Test:**
Tests whether any of a group of autocorrelations are different from zero:

$$Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{h}\frac{\hat{\rho}(k)^2}{T-k} \sim \chi^2(h)$$

### 3.6 Practical Interpretation Guide

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">

ACF and PACF are diagnostic tools for model identification:

</Callout>

1. **Examine ACF first:**
   - Slow decay suggests non-stationarity (difference the series)
   - Seasonal spikes suggest seasonal differencing needed

2. **After achieving stationarity:**
   - ACF cuts off → MA model
   - PACF cuts off → AR model
   - Both tail off → ARMA model

3. **For seasonal patterns:**
   - Look at lags $s, 2s, 3s, ...$ for seasonal structure

## 4. ARIMA Models: The Statistical Baseline

### 4.1 The ARIMA Framework

ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) models, developed by Box and Jenkins (1970), provide a systematic approach to time series forecasting.

**ARIMA(p,d,q) Model:**

$$\nabla^d x_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i \nabla^d x_{t-i} + \epsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j \epsilon_{t-j}$$

Using backshift notation:
$$(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i B^i)(1 - B)^d x_t = c + (1 + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j B^j)\epsilon_t$$

Or more compactly:
$$\phi(B)\nabla^d x_t = c + \theta(B)\epsilon_t$$

### 4.2 Component Breakdown

#### 4.2.1 AR(p): Autoregressive Component

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \phi_2 x_{t-2} + ... + \phi_p x_{t-p} + \epsilon_t$$

**Stationarity Condition:**
All roots of the characteristic equation must lie outside the unit circle:
$$1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2 - ... - \phi_p z^p = 0$$

| AR(1) | AR(2) | Interpretation |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| $\phi_1 > 0$ | - | Positive autocorrelation |
| $\phi_1 < 0$ | - | Oscillating behavior |
| $|\phi_1| < 1$ | - | Stationary |
| - | $\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 < 0$ | Damped oscillations |

#### 4.2.2 MA(q): Moving Average Component

$$x_t = \mu + \epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \epsilon_{t-2} + ... + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q}$$

**Invertibility Condition:**
All roots of the MA characteristic equation must lie outside the unit circle:
$$1 + \theta_1 z + \theta_2 z^2 + ... + \theta_q z^q = 0$$

#### 4.2.3 I(d): Integration (Differencing)

The number of differences required to achieve stationarity.

### 4.3 Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA)

For series with seasonal patterns:

**SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)s:**
$$\Phi_P(B^s)\phi_p(B)\nabla^d\nabla_s^D x_t = \Theta_Q(B^s)\theta_q(B)\epsilon_t$$

Where:
- $s$ = seasonal period
- $P, D, Q$ = seasonal AR, differencing, MA orders
- $\Phi_P(B^s)$ = seasonal AR polynomial
- $\Theta_Q(B^s)$ = seasonal MA polynomial

### 4.4 Model Identification Procedure

**Step 1: Check Stationarity**
- Apply ADF and KPSS tests
- Difference until stationary (determine $d$)

**Step 2: Examine ACF/PACF**
- Identify AR and MA orders from patterns
- Check for seasonal patterns

**Step 3: Estimate Parameters**
- Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
- Conditional Least Squares

**Step 4: Diagnostic Checking**
- Ljung-Box test on residuals
- Normality of residuals
- No remaining autocorrelation

### 4.5 Parameter Estimation

**Maximum Likelihood Estimation:**
Maximize the log-likelihood function:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{T}{2}\log(\sigma^2_\epsilon) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2_\epsilon}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_t^2$$

**Information Criteria for Model Selection:**

| Criterion | Formula | Penalty for Complexity |
|-----------|---------|----------------------|
| AIC | $-2\log(\mathcal{L}) + 2k$ | Moderate |
| AICc | AIC + $\frac{2k(k+1)}{T-k-1}$ | Adjusted for small samples |
| BIC | $-2\log(\mathcal{L}) + k\log(T)$ | Strong |
| HQIC | $-2\log(\mathcal{L}) + 2k\log(\log(T))$ | Moderate-strong |

Where $k = p + q + P + Q + 1$ (number of parameters).

### 4.6 Forecasting with ARIMA

**Point Forecasts:**
Minimum mean squared error forecast:
$$\hat{x}_{T+h|T} = E[x_{T+h} | x_1, ..., x_T]$$

**Forecast Variance:**
$$\text{Var}(e_{T+h|T}) = \sigma^2_\epsilon \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2$$

Where $\psi_j$ are the weights from the infinite MA representation.

**Prediction Intervals:**
$$\hat{x}_{T+h|T} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\text{Var}(e_{T+h|T})}$$

### 4.7 ARIMA as a Baseline for Deep Learning

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">

ARIMA models serve as essential baselines:

</Callout>

1. **Benchmark Performance:** Any sophisticated model should outperform a well-tuned ARIMA
2. **Model Validation:** If deep learning doesn't beat ARIMA, investigate why
3. **Feature Engineering:** ARIMA residuals can inform feature design
4. **Hybrid Approaches:** Combine ARIMA with neural networks

**When ARIMA Excels:**
- Small datasets
- Clear seasonal patterns
- Linear relationships
- Interpretability requirements

**When Deep Learning Excels:**
- Large datasets
- Complex nonlinear patterns
- Multiple input series
- Long-term dependencies

## 5. Seasonal Decomposition

### 5.1 Classical Decomposition

Time series can be decomposed into components:

**Additive Model:**
$$x_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

**Multiplicative Model:**
$$x_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$$

Where:
- $T_t$ = Trend component
- $S_t$ = Seasonal component
- $R_t$ = Residual/irregular component

### 5.2 Moving Average Trend Estimation

**Centered Moving Average:**
$$\hat{T}_t = \frac{1}{2m+1}\sum_{j=-m}^{m}x_{t+j}$$

For seasonal period $s$:
- If $s$ is odd: Use $m = (s-1)/2$
- If $s$ is even: Use 2×$s$ moving average

### 5.3 Seasonal Component Estimation

**Step 1:** Detrend the series
- Additive: $x_t - \hat{T}_t$
- Multiplicative: $x_t / \hat{T}_t$

**Step 2:** Average by season
$$\hat{S}_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{t \in \text{season } k}(x_t - \hat{T}_t)$$

**Step 3:** Normalize so seasonal components sum to zero (additive) or average to one (multiplicative)

### 5.4 STL Decomposition (Seasonal and Trend using Loess)

STL is a robust, versatile decomposition method using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS).

**Algorithm Overview:**
1. **Inner Loop:**
   - Detrend and extract seasonal component
   - Apply LOESS smoothing to seasonal sub-series
   - Subtract seasonal to get trend

2. **Outer Loop:**
   - Compute residuals
   - Apply robustness weights based on residual magnitude
   - Repeat inner loop with weights

**Advantages of STL:**
- Handles any seasonal period
- Robust to outliers
- Trend and seasonal smoothness adjustable
- Can handle changing seasonality

**Parameters:**
| Parameter | Description | Typical Values |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| $s$ | Seasonal period | Data-dependent |
| $n_t$ | Trend smoothness | Odd integer |
| $n_s$ | Seasonal smoothness | Odd integer |
| $n_i$ | Inner loop iterations | 1-5 |
| $n_o$ | Outer loop iterations | 0-15 |

### 5.5 X-13ARIMA-SEATS

The U.S. Census Bureau's official seasonal adjustment program:

**Features:**
- RegARIMA modeling (ARIMA with regression effects)
- Automatic outlier detection
- Trading day and holiday adjustment
- Model-based SEATS decomposition

**When to Use:**
- Official statistics publication
- Economic time series
- When comparability with official data matters

### 5.6 Practical Seasonal Adjustment Workflow

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL

# STL decomposition
stl = STL(series, period=12, robust=True)
result = stl.fit()

trend = result.trend
seasonal = result.seasonal
residual = result.resid

# Seasonally adjusted series
adjusted = series - seasonal  # Additive
```

## 6. Windowing Strategies: Mathematical Foundations

### 6.1 The Windowing Problem

Windowing transforms sequential data into supervised learning format:

**Input:** Time series $\{x_1, x_2, ..., x_T\}$
**Output:** Pairs $\{(X_i, y_i)\}$ where $X_i$ is input window, $y_i$ is target

### 6.2 Window Types

#### 6.2.1 Sliding Window (Rolling Window)

**Definition:**
Window advances by one time step each iteration.

**Input Window:**
$$X_t = (x_{t-w+1}, x_{t-w+2}, ..., x_t)$$

**Target:**
$$y_t = x_{t+h}$$

Where $w$ = window size, $h$ = forecast horizon.

**Number of Windows:**
$$N_{windows} = T - w - h + 1$$

**Example (w=3, h=1):**
| Window | Input | Target |
|--------|-------|--------|
| 1 | $[x_1, x_2, x_3]$ | $x_4$ |
| 2 | $[x_2, x_3, x_4]$ | $x_5$ |
| 3 | $[x_3, x_4, x_5]$ | $x_6$ |

#### 6.2.2 Expanding Window

Window grows over time, incorporating all historical data:

$$X_t = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_t)$$

**Use Cases:**
- When all historical context matters
- Cumulative statistics
- Online learning scenarios

#### 6.2.3 Tumbling Window (Non-overlapping)

Windows do not overlap:

$$X_k = (x_{(k-1)w+1}, ..., x_{kw})$$

**Number of Windows:**
$$N_{windows} = \lfloor T/w \rfloor$$

### 6.3 Window Size Selection

**Theoretical Considerations:**

| Factor | Smaller Window | Larger Window |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| Training samples | More | Fewer |
| Context captured | Limited | Extensive |
| Computational cost | Lower | Higher |
| Non-stationarity | Less impact | More impact |
| Long-term patterns | May miss | Captures |

**Heuristics:**
- Minimum: At least 2× seasonal period
- For ARIMA: At least 10 observations per parameter
- For deep learning: Thousands to millions of windows
- Cross-validation for optimal selection

### 6.4 Feature Engineering from Windows

#### 6.4.1 Statistical Features

For window $W = (x_{t-w+1}, ..., x_t)$:

| Feature | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| Mean | $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{w}\sum_{i=1}^{w}x_i$ |
| Std Dev | $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{w}\sum_{i=1}^{w}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}$ |
| Min/Max | $\min(W), \max(W)$ |
| Range | $\max(W) - \min(W)$ |
| Skewness | $\frac{1}{w}\sum_{i=1}^{w}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sigma}\right)^3$ |
| Kurtosis | $\frac{1}{w}\sum_{i=1}^{w}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sigma}\right)^4 - 3$ |

#### 6.4.2 Temporal Features

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Time of day | Hour, minute cyclical encoding |
| Day of week | 0-6 with cyclical encoding |
| Day of month | 1-31 |
| Month | 1-12 with cyclical encoding |
| Quarter | 1-4 |
| Is weekend | Binary |
| Is holiday | Binary |

**Cyclical Encoding:**
$$x_{sin} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{period}\right), \quad x_{cos} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{period}\right)$$

#### 6.4.3 Lag Features

Direct inclusion of past values:
$$X_t = (x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, ..., x_{t-w})$$

#### 6.4.4 Rolling Statistics

Rolling mean with different windows:
$$\text{MA}_k(t) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{t-i}$$

Exponentially weighted moving average:
$$\text{EWMA}_\alpha(t) = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)\text{EWMA}_\alpha(t-1)$$

### 6.5 Windowing for Different Architectures

#### 6.5.1 For RNNs/LSTMs

**3D Input Shape:** $(batch\_size, timesteps, features)$

```python
# Window shape for univariate LSTM
window_size = 30
X = []  # Shape: (n_samples, 30, 1)
y = []  # Shape: (n_samples,)

for i in range(len(series) - window_size):
    X.append(series[i:i+window_size])
    y.append(series[i+window_size])
```

#### 6.5.2 For 1D CNNs

Same window structure as RNNs; CNN learns local patterns.

#### 6.5.3 For Transformers

Include positional encoding in windows:
$$PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin(pos/10000^{2i/d_{model}})$$

### 6.6 Data Leakage Prevention

**Critical Rule:** Never use future information to predict the past.

**Common Leakage Sources:**

| Source | Problem | Solution |
|--------|---------|----------|
| Global normalization | Uses future mean/std | Use rolling/expanding statistics |
| Feature selection | Uses full dataset | Select on training only |
| Train/test split | Random split breaks time | Use temporal split |
| Cross-validation | Random folds | Time Series Split |

**Proper Train/Validation/Test Split:**

```python
[Training: 60%][Validation: 20%][Test: 20%]
|---------------> Time ------------>|
```

**Time Series Cross-Validation:**

```python
Fold 1: [Train][Val         ]
Fold 2: [Train     ][Val    ]
Fold 3: [Train          ][Val]
```

## 7. Forecasting Strategies: Direct, Recursive, and Multi-Output

### 7.1 Problem Formulation

Given historical data $\{x_1, ..., x_T\}$, predict future values $\{x_{T+1}, ..., x_{T+H}\}$ where $H$ is the forecast horizon.

### 7.2 Direct Forecasting (Multi-Model)

**Approach:** Train separate model for each horizon.

**Model for horizon h:**
$$\hat{x}_{T+h} = f_h(x_T, x_{T-1}, ..., x_{T-w+1})$$

**Advantages:**
- No error accumulation
- Each model optimized for specific horizon
- Captures horizon-specific patterns

**Disadvantages:**
- Computational cost: H models to train
- No parameter sharing
- May produce incoherent forecasts

**Mathematical Formulation:**
$$\min_{\theta_h} \sum_{t=w}^{T-h} L(x_{t+h}, f_h(x_t, ..., x_{t-w+1}; \theta_h))$$

For each $h \in \{1, ..., H\}$

### 7.3 Recursive Forecasting (Iterative)

**Approach:** Single model, use predictions as inputs for next step.

**One-step model:**
$$\hat{x}_{t+1} = f(x_t, x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-w+1})$$

**Multi-step prediction:**
$$\hat{x}_{T+2} = f(\hat{x}_{T+1}, x_T, ..., x_{T-w+2})$$
$$\hat{x}_{T+h} = f(\hat{x}_{T+h-1}, ..., \hat{x}_{T+1}, x_T, ..., x_{T-w+h})$$

**Advantages:**
- Single model to train
- Parameter efficiency
- Natural autoregressive structure

**Disadvantages:**
- Error accumulation
- Bias toward one-step performance
- Cannot recover from early errors

**Error Propagation:**
$$\text{Var}(\hat{x}_{T+h}) \approx h \cdot \sigma^2_\epsilon$$

### 7.4 Multi-Output Forecasting (Sequence-to-Sequence)

**Approach:** Single model predicts entire horizon at once.

$$\hat{x}_{T+1:T+H} = f(x_T, x_{T-1}, ..., x_{T-w+1})$$

Output: Vector of length H

**Advantages:**
- Captures dependencies between horizons
- No error accumulation
- Single model

**Disadvantages:**
- More complex model
- Requires more data
- May underperform at longer horizons

**Loss Function:**
$$L = \sum_{h=1}^{H} w_h \cdot L_h(x_{T+h}, \hat{x}_{T+h})$$

Where $w_h$ are horizon-specific weights.

### 7.5 Comparison Summary

| Aspect | Direct | Recursive | Multi-Output |
|--------|--------|-----------|--------------|
| Models | H | 1 | 1 |
| Error accumulation | No | Yes | No |
| Training time | High | Low | Medium |
| Horizon coherence | No | Yes | Yes |
| Best for | Long horizons | Short horizons | Medium horizons |
| Data efficiency | Low | High | Medium |

### 7.6 Advanced Strategies

#### 7.6.1 DirRec (Direct+Recursive)

Hybrid approach using direct models with recursive inputs.

#### 7.6.2 MIMO (Multi-Input Multi-Output)

Predict multiple series simultaneously:
$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{T+1:T+H} = f(\mathbf{x}_T, ..., \mathbf{x}_{T-w+1})$$

Where $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$

#### 7.6.3 Encoder-Decoder Architecture

**Encoder:** Processes historical context
$$\mathbf{h} = \text{Encoder}(x_T, ..., x_{T-w+1})$$

**Decoder:** Generates forecasts
$$\hat{x}_{T+h} = \text{Decoder}(\mathbf{h}, \hat{x}_{T+h-1}, ...)$$

Teacher forcing during training:
$$\hat{x}_{T+h} = \text{Decoder}(\mathbf{h}, x_{T+h-1}, ...)$$

### 7.7 Loss Functions for Multi-Horizon Forecasting

| Loss | Formula | Use Case |
|------|---------|----------|
| MSE | $\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}(x_{T+h} - \hat{x}_{T+h})^2$ | Standard |
| MAE | $\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}|x_{T+h} - \hat{x}_{T+h}|$ | Robust |
| RMSE | $\sqrt{\text{MSE}}$ | Same units as data |
| MAPE | $\frac{100\%}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left|\frac{x_{T+h} - \hat{x}_{T+h}}{x_{T+h}}\right|$ | Scale-independent |
| sMAPE | $\frac{200\%}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{|x_{T+h} - \hat{x}_{T+h}|}{|x_{T+h}| + |\hat{x}_{T+h}|}$ | Symmetric |
| Quantile | $\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\rho_\tau(x_{T+h} - \hat{x}_{T+h})$ | Probabilistic |

## 8. Deep Learning Architectures for Time Series

### 8.1 1D Convolutional Neural Networks

#### 8.1.1 1D Convolution Operation

$$y_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k \cdot x_{t+k} + b$$

Where $K$ is the kernel size.

#### 8.1.2 Dilated Convolutions

Expand receptive field exponentially:

$$y_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k \cdot x_{t + d \cdot k}$$

Where $d$ is the dilation rate.

**Receptive Field:**
$$RF = (K-1) \cdot d + 1$$

#### 8.1.3 Causal Convolutions

Ensure predictions only use past information:

$$y_t = f(x_t, x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-K+1})$$

No dependence on $x_{t+1}, x_{t+2}, ...$

### 8.2 Recurrent Neural Networks

#### 8.2.1 Vanilla RNN

**Hidden State Update:**
$$\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h)$$

**Output:**
$$\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y$$

**Problem:** Vanishing/exploding gradients for long sequences.

#### 8.2.2 Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

**Forget Gate:**
$$\mathbf{f}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f)$$

**Input Gate:**
$$\mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i)$$
$$\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_C \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_C)$$

**Cell State Update:**
$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t$$

**Output Gate:**
$$\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o)$$
$$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{C}_t)$$

**Key Innovation:** Cell state provides highway for gradient flow.

#### 8.2.3 Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

Simplified version with fewer gates:

**Update Gate:**
$$\mathbf{z}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_z \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t])$$

**Reset Gate:**
$$\mathbf{r}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_r \cdot [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t])$$

**Candidate Activation:**
$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W} \cdot [\mathbf{r}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t])$$

**Hidden State:**
$$\mathbf{h}_t = (1 - \mathbf{z}_t) \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$$

**LSTM vs GRU:**
| Feature | LSTM | GRU |
|---------|------|-----|
| Gates | 3 | 2 |
| Parameters | More | Fewer |
| Training speed | Slower | Faster |
| Performance | Often better | Often comparable |

### 8.3 Bidirectional RNNs

Process sequence in both directions:

$$\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t = \text{RNN}(\mathbf{x}_t, \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1})$$
$$\overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t = \text{RNN}(\mathbf{x}_t, \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1})$$

$$\mathbf{h}_t = [\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t; \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t]$$

<Callout type="note" title="Note">

Only applicable when future context is available (not for forecasting).

</Callout>

### 8.4 Attention Mechanisms

#### 8.4.1 Self-Attention for Time Series

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

For time series:
- $Q, K, V$ derived from input sequence
- Causal masking for forecasting

#### 8.4.2 Multi-Head Attention

$$\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, ..., \text{head}_h)W^O$$

Where:
$$\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V)$$

### 8.5 Architecture Selection Guide

| Scenario | Recommended Architecture |
|----------|------------------------|
| Short sequences (&lt;100) | LSTM/GRU |
| Long sequences (>1000) | TCN, Transformer |
| Local patterns important | 1D CNN, TCN |
| Long-term dependencies | LSTM, TCN, Transformer |
| Multivariate series | LSTM with attention, N-BEATS |
| Interpretability needed | N-BEATS, ARIMA |
| Large dataset | Transformer, DeepAR |

## 9. Probabilistic Forecasting

### 9.1 Beyond Point Forecasts

Point forecasts provide single values; probabilistic forecasts provide full distributions.

**Why Probabilistic Forecasting:**
- Uncertainty quantification
- Risk assessment
- Decision making under uncertainty
- Inventory optimization
- Resource planning

### 9.2 Types of Probabilistic Forecasts

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Prediction Intervals** | Range with specified coverage | 95% PI: [10, 20] |
| **Quantile Forecasts** | Specific quantiles | Q(0.9) = 18 |
| **Full Distribution** | Complete probability distribution | $X_{T+h} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ |
| **Scenarios** | Multiple possible futures | 100 sample paths |

### 9.3 Quantile Regression

**Loss Function (Pinball Loss):**
$$\rho_\tau(y, \hat{y}) = \begin{cases}
\tau(y - \hat{y}) & \text{if } y \geq \hat{y} \\
(1-\tau)(\hat{y} - y) & \text{if } y < \hat{y}
\end{cases}$$

**Properties:**
- Minimizing pinball loss yields $\tau$-th quantile
- Asymmetric: different penalties for over/under-prediction
- Robust to outliers

**Multi-Quantile Model:**
Train model to output multiple quantiles simultaneously:
$$\hat{\mathbf{q}} = [\hat{q}_{0.1}, \hat{q}_{0.5}, \hat{q}_{0.9}]$$

**Quantile Crossing Problem:**
Ensure $\hat{q}_{\tau_1} \leq \hat{q}_{\tau_2}$ for $\tau_1 < \tau_2$

Solutions:
- Sort quantiles post-hoc
- Use monotonic output layers

### 9.4 Parametric Probabilistic Models

#### 9.4.1 Gaussian Distribution

$$p(x_{T+h}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{T+h}, \sigma^2_{T+h})$$

Model outputs: $(\mu, \sigma)$

**Negative Log-Likelihood Loss:**
$$L = -\log p(x | \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$$

#### 9.4.2 Mixture Density Networks

Mixture of Gaussians:
$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$$

Model outputs: $\{(\pi_k, \mu_k, \sigma_k)\}_{k=1}^{K}$

Constraints:
- $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$
- $\sigma_k > 0$

#### 9.4.3 Student's t-Distribution

Heavier tails than Gaussian:
$$p(x) = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2})}\left(1 + \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\nu\sigma^2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}$$

More robust to outliers; $\nu$ = degrees of freedom.

### 9.5 DeepAR: Probabilistic RNN

Amazon's DeepAR model combines RNNs with probabilistic forecasting.

**Architecture:**
1. RNN processes historical data: $\mathbf{h}_t = \text{RNN}(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{h}_{t-1})$
2. Output parameters of distribution: $(\mu_t, \sigma_t) = g(\mathbf{h}_t)$
3. Sample next value: $x_{t+1} \sim p(\cdot | \mu_t, \sigma_t)$
4. Feed sample back as input (autoregressive)

**Training:**
Maximize likelihood over training sequences.

**Inference:**
Generate multiple sample paths (Monte Carlo).

### 9.6 Conformal Prediction

Distribution-free uncertainty quantification.

**Algorithm:**
1. Compute non-conformity scores on calibration set
2. Find threshold $\hat{q}$ as $(1-\alpha)(1 + 1/n)$-th quantile
3. Prediction set: $\{y : s(x, y) \leq \hat{q}\}$

**Guarantee:** Coverage $\geq 1-\alpha$ (marginal)

### 9.7 Evaluation of Probabilistic Forecasts

#### 9.7.1 Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)

$$\text{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(F(x) - \mathbb{1}_{[y,\infty)}(x))^2 dx$$

For Gaussian:
$$\text{CRPS}(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma), y) = \sigma\left(z(2\Phi(z) - 1) + 2\phi(z) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)$$

Where $z = (y - \mu)/\sigma$

#### 9.7.2 Quantile Score

$$QS_\tau = \frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_\tau(y_t, \hat{q}_\tau(t))$$

#### 9.7.3 Coverage and Width

| Metric | Formula | Target |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Coverage | $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \mathbb{1}_{[L_t, U_t]}(y_t)$ | $\approx 1-\alpha$ |
| Average Width | $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t (U_t - L_t)$ | Minimize |

## 10. Anomaly Detection in Time Series

### 10.1 Types of Anomalies

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Point Anomalies** | Individual unusual observations | Sensor spike |
| **Contextual Anomalies** | Unusual in context but not globally | High temperature in winter |
| **Collective Anomalies** | Sequence of unusual values | Gradual drift |
| **Seasonal Anomalies** | Unusual within seasonal pattern | Holiday sales spike |

### 10.2 Statistical Approaches

#### 10.2.1 Z-Score Method

$$z_t = \frac{x_t - \mu}{\sigma}$$

Anomaly if $|z_t| > \tau$ (typically $\tau = 3$)

**Rolling Z-Score:**
$$z_t = \frac{x_t - \bar{x}_{t-w:t}}{\sigma_{t-w:t}}$$

#### 10.2.2 Modified Z-Score

Using median and MAD (Median Absolute Deviation):

$$M_t = 0.6745 \cdot \frac{x_t - \text{median}}{\text{MAD}}$$

More robust to outliers.

#### 10.2.3 IQR Method

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$$

Anomaly if:
$$x_t < Q_1 - 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR} \quad \text{or} \quad x_t > Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}$$

### 10.3 Model-Based Approaches

#### 10.3.1 Forecasting-Based

1. Train forecasting model
2. Compute prediction errors: $e_t = x_t - \hat{x}_t$
3. Anomaly if $|e_t| > \tau$

#### 10.3.2 STL-Based Decomposition

1. Decompose: $x_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$
2. Analyze residuals $R_t$
3. Anomaly if $|R_t| > \tau \cdot \text{MAD}(R)$

### 10.4 Deep Learning Approaches

#### 10.4.1 Autoencoders

**Architecture:**
- Encoder: Compresses input to latent representation
- Decoder: Reconstructs input from latent space

**Anomaly Score:**
$$\text{score}(x) = \|x - \text{Decoder}(\text{Encoder}(x))\|^2$$

**Variants:**
- LSTM Autoencoder (for sequences)
- Variational Autoencoder (probabilistic)
- Convolutional Autoencoder

#### 10.4.2 Isolation Forest

Randomly partition data; anomalies isolated in fewer splits.

**Anomaly Score:**
$$s(x, n) = 2^{-\frac{E(h(x))}{c(n)}}$$

Where $h(x)$ is path length and $c(n)$ is average path length.

#### 10.4.3 One-Class SVM

Learn boundary of normal data:

$$\min_{w, \rho} \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 - \rho + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_i \xi_i$$

Subject to: $w \cdot \phi(x_i) \geq \rho - \xi_i$

### 10.5 Evaluation Metrics

| Metric | Formula | Use Case |
|--------|---------|----------|
| Precision | $\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$ | Minimize false alarms |
| Recall | $\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ | Catch all anomalies |
| F1-Score | $2\frac{Precision \cdot Recall}{Precision + Recall}$ | Balance |
| AUC-ROC | Area under ROC curve | Threshold-independent |
| AUC-PR | Area under Precision-Recall curve | Imbalanced data |

### 10.6 Common Pitfalls

**Pitfall 1: Label Contamination**
- Training data may contain unlabeled anomalies
- Solution: Robust training, outlier removal

**Pitfall 2: Concept Drift**
- Normal behavior changes over time
- Solution: Online learning, adaptive thresholds

**Pitfall 3: Seasonal Confusion**
- Seasonal peaks flagged as anomalies
- Solution: Seasonal decomposition first

## 11. Multivariate Time Series

### 11.1 Problem Definition

Multiple related time series observed simultaneously:

$$\mathbf{X}_t = [x_{t,1}, x_{t,2}, ..., x_{t,d}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

Where $d$ is the number of variables.

### 11.2 Cross-Correlation

**Cross-Correlation Function:**
$$\rho_{xy}(k) = \frac{\text{Cov}(x_t, y_{t-k})}{\sigma_x \sigma_y}$$

**Cross-Correlation Matrix:**
$$\mathbf{R}(k) = \begin{pmatrix}
\rho_{11}(k) & \rho_{12}(k) & \cdots & \rho_{1d}(k) \\
\rho_{21}(k) & \rho_{22}(k) & \cdots & \rho_{2d}(k) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\rho_{d1}(k) & \rho_{d2}(k) & \cdots & \rho_{dd}(k)
\end{pmatrix}$$

### 11.3 Vector Autoregression (VAR)

**VAR(p) Model:**
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{c} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{x}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$$

Where:
- $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the constant vector
- $\mathbf{A}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are coefficient matrices
- $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$

**VAR(1) in Matrix Form:**
$$\begin{pmatrix}
x_{t,1} \\
x_{t,2} \\
\vdots \\
x_{t,d}
\end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
c_1 \\
c_2 \\
\vdots \\
c_d
\end{pmatrix} +
\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1d} \\
a_{21} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2d} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
a_{d1} & a_{d2} & \cdots & a_{dd}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_{t-1,1} \\
x_{t-1,2} \\
\vdots \\
x_{t-1,d}
\end{pmatrix} +
\begin{pmatrix}
\epsilon_{t,1} \\
\epsilon_{t,2} \\
\vdots \\
\epsilon_{t,d}
\end{pmatrix}$$

### 11.4 Granger Causality

Tests whether one time series helps predict another.

**Null Hypothesis:** Series $y$ does not Granger-cause series $x$

**Test:**
1. Estimate restricted model: $x_t$ on lags of $x$
2. Estimate unrestricted model: $x_t$ on lags of $x$ and $y$
3. F-test: Are lags of $y$ jointly significant?

**Limitations:**
- Not true causality (predictive relationship)
- Sensitive to omitted variables
- Requires stationarity

### 11.5 Cointegration

When non-stationary series share a common stochastic trend.

**Definition:**
Series $\mathbf{x}_t$ are cointegrated if:
1. Each $x_{t,i}$ is I(1) (integrated of order 1)
2. There exists $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ such that $\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_t$ is I(0)

**Engle-Granger Test:**
1. Test each series for unit root
2. Estimate: $x_{t,1} = \alpha + \beta x_{t,2} + \epsilon_t$
3. Test residuals for unit root

**Vector Error Correction Model (VECM):**
$$\Delta \mathbf{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_i\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$$

Where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ is the error correction term.

### 11.6 Deep Learning for Multivariate Series

#### 11.6.1 LSTM with Multiple Inputs

```python
# Input shape: (batch, timesteps, features)
model = Sequential([
    LSTM(64, input_shape=(timesteps, n_features)),
    Dense(n_outputs)
])
```

#### 11.6.2 Graph Neural Networks for MT

When variables have known relationships:

$$\mathbf{H}^{(l+1)} = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{H}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})$$

Where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I}$ is the adjacency matrix with self-loops.

### 11.7 Dimensionality Reduction

#### 11.7.1 Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

$$\mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{W}^T(\mathbf{x}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu})$$

Where $\mathbf{W}$ contains eigenvectors of covariance matrix.

#### 11.7.2 Dynamic Factor Models

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{f}_t + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$$

Where $\mathbf{f}_t$ are common factors and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ is the loading matrix.

## 12. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN)

### 12.1 Introduction

TCNs, introduced by Bai, Kolter, and Koltun (2018), offer a CNN-based alternative to RNNs for sequence modeling.

**Key Advantages:**
- Parallel processing (unlike sequential RNNs)
- Flexible receptive field size
- Stable gradients
- Lower memory requirements for training

### 12.2 Core Components

#### 12.2.1 Causal Convolutions

Ensure no information leakage from future to past:

$$\text{Output}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \text{Filter}_k \cdot \text{Input}_{t-k}$$

**Implementation:** Padding on the left only.

#### 12.2.2 Dilated Convolutions

Exponentially increase receptive field:

$$F(s) = (x * d f)(s) = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} f(i) \cdot x_{s - d \cdot i}$$

Where $d$ is the dilation factor.

**Receptive Field Growth:**
With $L$ layers and dilation $d_i = 2^i$:
$$RF = 1 + 2 \cdot (K - 1) \cdot (2^L - 1)$$

<Callout type="note" title="Example">

| Layer | Dilation | Receptive Field Contribution | |-------|----------|------------------------------| | 1 | 1 | $K$ | | 2 | 2 | $2(K-1)$ | | 3 | 4 | $4(K-1)$ | | 4 | 8 | $8(K-1)$ |

</Callout>

Total: $RF = 1 + (K-1)(1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 1 + 15(K-1)$

#### 12.2.3 Residual Connections

Address vanishing gradients in deep networks:

$$\mathbf{o} = \text{Activation}(\mathbf{x} + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{W}))$$

Where $\mathcal{F}$ is the convolutional block.

**TCN Residual Block:**
```python
Input
  |
  +---> [Conv1D] ---> [WeightNorm] ---> [ReLU] ---> [Dropout]
  |           |
  |           v
  |       [Conv1D] ---> [WeightNorm] ---> [ReLU] ---> [Dropout]
  |           |
  +-----------+---> [1x1 Conv if channels differ]
              |
              v
           [Output]
```

### 12.3 TCN Architecture

```python
Input Sequence
     |
     v
[TCN Block 1] - dilation=1
     |
     v
[TCN Block 2] - dilation=2
     |
     v
[TCN Block 3] - dilation=4
     |
     v
[TCN Block 4] - dilation=8
     |
     v
   [Dense]
     |
     v
   Output
```

### 12.4 Mathematical Formulation

**Single TCN Layer:**
$$\mathbf{h}^{(l)}_t = \text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)} *_{d_l} \mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}_t + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)$$

Where $*_{d_l}$ denotes dilated convolution with dilation $d_l = 2^{l-1}$.

**Full Network:**
$$\hat{y}_t = \mathbf{W}^{out} \cdot \mathbf{h}^{(L)}_t + b^{out}$$

### 12.5 TCN vs LSTM Comparison

| Aspect | TCN | LSTM |
|--------|-----|------|
| Parallelism | Yes | No |
| Receptive field | Adjustable | Theoretically infinite |
| Training speed | Faster | Slower |
| Memory during training | Lower | Higher |
| Long-term dependencies | Good | Can struggle |
| Variable length inputs | Padding needed | Natural handling |
| Interpretability | Filter visualization | Gate analysis |

### 12.6 Advanced TCN Variants

#### 12.6.1 Gated TCN

Introduce gating mechanism:

$$\mathbf{h} = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{f} * \mathbf{x}) \odot \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{g} * \mathbf{x})$$

Where $\mathbf{W}_f$ is filter and $\mathbf{W}_g$ is gate.

#### 12.6.2 Attention-Augmented TCN

Add self-attention to capture global dependencies:

$$\text{Output} = \text{TCN}(\mathbf{x}) + \text{Attention}(\mathbf{x})$$

### 12.7 Practical Considerations

**Hyperparameter Selection:**

| Parameter | Effect | Typical Range |
|-----------|--------|---------------|
| Kernel size | Local context captured | 2-7 |
| Number of layers | Receptive field depth | 4-10 |
| Filters per layer | Model capacity | 32-256 |
| Dilation base | Receptive field growth | 2 |

**Receptive Field Calculation:**
```python
def receptive_field(kernel_size, n_layers, dilation_base=2):
    return 1 + (kernel_size - 1) * (dilation_base**n_layers - 1)

# Example: kernel=3, layers=8
rf = receptive_field(3, 8)  # 511
```

Hierarchical interpolation for time series:

## Common Misconceptions

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: Stationarity means constant values">
Stationarity does **not** mean the series stays flat or near a constant. A stationary series still fluctuates — often wildly. Stationarity means the *statistical properties* (mean, variance, autocorrelation structure) remain constant over time. White noise is stationary despite being unpredictable; a slowly rising stock price is non-stationary despite looking smooth.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: LSTMs always beat ARIMA">
For simple, well-structured series with strong linear autocorrelation, a properly tuned ARIMA (or ETS) frequently **outperforms** deep learning models. The M4 Competition (2018) showed that pure ML methods underperformed hybrid and statistical approaches in many categories. LSTMs and TCNs shine with complex nonlinear patterns, multivariate inputs, and large datasets — but never skip the classical baseline.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: More historical data is always better">
In time series, older data may reflect a different regime (concept drift, structural breaks). Including 20 years of data for a process that fundamentally changed 3 years ago can *hurt* model performance. Use domain knowledge to select an appropriate training window, and consider regime-detection techniques (e.g., CUSUM tests, Markov-switching models) to identify breakpoints.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: Differencing always fixes non-stationarity">
Over-differencing is a common trap. Each round of differencing removes information and can introduce artificial MA structure. If ADF says stationary but KPSS says non-stationary after $d=1$, the issue may be a deterministic trend (use detrending, not differencing) or a structural break — not insufficient differencing. Always use both ADF and KPSS as complementary tests.
</Callout>

## Key Takeaways

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
- **Stationarity is non-negotiable:** Apply both ADF (null: unit root) and KPSS (null: stationary) tests before modelling. Conflicting results indicate deterministic trends or structural breaks requiring specific treatment — not more differencing.
- **Classical baselines remain competitive:** A well-tuned ARIMA or ETS model is the minimum bar your deep learning model must clear. If it doesn't, the issue is likely data preparation or architecture choice, not model capacity.
- **Window size determines learnable patterns:** The sliding window defines your model's temporal receptive field. Too short and you miss seasonal cycles; too long and you dilute recent signal with stale history. Match window length to the dominant periodicity in your ACF/PACF plots.
- **Probabilistic forecasting is essential for decisions:** Point forecasts alone are insufficient. Quantile regression, Monte Carlo dropout, or mixture density networks provide prediction intervals that quantify uncertainty — critical for downstream decision-making in finance, energy, and healthcare.
</Callout>

## Key Insights Summary

### Insight 1: Stationarity is Foundation
Always test and achieve stationarity before modeling. Non-stationary series lead to spurious relationships and unreliable forecasts.

### Insight 2: ARIMA as Baseline
A well-tuned ARIMA model is a powerful baseline. If your deep learning model doesn't beat it, investigate your architecture and training procedure.

### Insight 3: Windowing Matters
The choice of window size and strategy fundamentally affects what patterns your model can learn. Match window size to the temporal scale of patterns you want to capture.

### Insight 4: Uncertainty is Essential
Point forecasts are insufficient for decision-making. Always provide prediction intervals or full distributions when possible.

### Insight 5: Architecture Selection
No single architecture is best for all problems. Consider sequence length, pattern complexity, data size, and interpretability needs.

## Common Pitfalls Summary

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Data leakage | Using future information | Temporal splits, rolling statistics |
| Over-differencing | Aggressive stationarity pursuit | Check ACF, use KPSS alongside ADF |
| Ignoring seasonality | Focus on trend only | Decompose first, model components |
| Wrong evaluation metric | Using standard CV | Time series cross-validation |
| Overfitting to noise | Complex models, small data | Regularization, simpler models |
| Ignoring scale | Different series magnitudes | Normalization, scaling |
| Forecast horizon mismatch | Training for wrong horizon | Match training to deployment |

## References

### Foundational Papers

- **Box, G. E. P. & Jenkins, G. M. (1970)** — *Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control.* The foundational text establishing the ARIMA methodology and the iterative identification-estimation-diagnostic approach that remains standard practice.
- **Hochreiter, S. & Schmidhuber, J. (1997)** — *Long Short-Term Memory.* Introduced the LSTM architecture with gating mechanisms that solve the vanishing gradient problem, enabling learning of long-range temporal dependencies.
- **Hyndman, R. J. & Athanasopoulos, G. (2021)** — *Forecasting: Principles and Practice* (3rd ed., OTexts). The standard modern reference for statistical forecasting covering ETS, ARIMA, and cross-validation for time series.
- **Bai, S., Kolter, J. Z. & Koltun, V. (2018)** — *An Empirical Evaluation of Generic Convolutional and Recurrent Networks for Sequence Modeling.* Demonstrated that TCNs can match or outperform LSTMs on many sequence tasks, establishing dilated causal convolutions as a serious alternative.
- **Oreshkin, B. N. et al. (2020)** — *N-BEATS: Neural Basis Expansion Analysis for Interpretable Time Series Forecasting.* Pure deep learning architecture without time-series-specific components, achieving state-of-the-art on M4 Competition via basis function expansion.
- **Zhou, H. et al. (2021)** — *Informer: Beyond Efficient Transformer for Long Sequence Time-Series Forecasting.* Introduced ProbSparse attention (O(L log L)) and self-attention distilling, enabling Transformers for long-horizon forecasting.
- **Wu, H. et al. (2021)** — *Autoformer: Decomposition Transformers with Auto-Correlation for Long-Term Series Forecasting.* Replaced self-attention with auto-correlation mechanism and introduced deep decomposition architecture for seasonal-trend separation.
- **Salinas, D. et al. (2020)** — *DeepAR: Probabilistic Forecasting with Autoregressive Recurrent Networks.* Trained a global autoregressive RNN across multiple related time series, outputting parametric probability distributions rather than point forecasts.
- **Chen, R. T. Q. et al. (2018)** — *Neural Ordinary Differential Equations.* Formulated continuous-depth neural networks via ODE solvers, naturally handling irregularly sampled time series without imputation.
- **Makridakis, S. et al. (2018)** — *The M4 Competition: 100,000 Time Series and 61 Forecasting Methods.* Comprehensive benchmark showing that hybrid statistical-ML methods outperformed pure deep learning, establishing the importance of classical baselines.

### Further Reading

- **Challu, C. et al. (2023)** — *N-HiTS: Neural Hierarchical Interpolation for Time Series Forecasting.* Extended N-BEATS with multi-rate sampling and hierarchical interpolation for improved long-horizon performance.
- **Zeng, A. et al. (2023)** — *Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?* Provocatively showed that simple linear models can outperform complex Transformers on several benchmarks, sparking important debate on appropriate model complexity.
- **Engle, R. F. (1982)** — *Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity with Estimates of the Variance of United Kingdom Inflation.* Introduced ARCH models for time-varying volatility, foundational for financial time series.
- **Cleveland, R. B. et al. (1990)** — *STL: A Seasonal-Trend Decomposition Procedure Based on LOESS.* The standard algorithm for robust seasonal decomposition, widely used in preprocessing pipelines.
- **Rangapuram, S. S. et al. (2018)** — *Deep State Space Models for Time Series Forecasting.* Combined state space models with deep learning to produce interpretable probabilistic forecasts with linear Gaussian structure.
