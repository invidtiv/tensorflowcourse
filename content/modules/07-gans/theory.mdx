## 1. Foundations of Adversarial Learning

### 1.1 The Generative Modeling Problem

Generative modeling aims to learn the underlying probability distribution $p_{data}(x)$ from a set of samples $\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from this distribution. The fundamental challenge is to construct a model that can:

- **Generate new samples** that appear to come from $p_{data}(x)$
- **Capture the full diversity** of the data distribution
- **Enable sampling** in a computationally efficient manner

Traditional approaches to generative modeling include:

| Approach | Method | Limitation |
|----------|--------|------------|
| Explicit density | Maximum likelihood estimation | Requires tractable density |
| Approximate density | Variational inference | Loose lower bounds |
| Implicit density | MCMC methods | Slow convergence |
| Implicit sampling | GANs | No explicit density |

### 1.2 The Adversarial Framework

GANs introduce a novel paradigm where two neural networks compete in a zero-sum game:

**Generator Network G:**
- Input: Random noise $z \sim p_z(z)$ (typically Gaussian)
- Output: Synthetic sample $G(z)$
- Objective: Fool the discriminator into classifying fake samples as real

**Discriminator Network D:**
- Input: Sample $x$ (real or generated)
- Output: Probability $D(x) \in [0,1]$ that $x$ is real
- Objective: Distinguish between real and fake samples

### 1.3 The Adversarial Objective Function

The original GAN objective (Goodfellow et al., 2014) is formulated as:

$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

This can be decomposed into two optimization problems:

**Discriminator's Objective (for fixed G):**
$$\max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

**Generator's Objective (for fixed D):**
$$\min_G V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

### Key Insight: Why This Works

The discriminator provides a **learned loss function** that adapts to the generator's current capabilities. Unlike fixed losses (MSE, cross-entropy), the discriminator loss evolves as the generator improves, providing increasingly sophisticated feedback.

## 2. Minimax Game Theory and Nash Equilibrium

### 2.1 Game-Theoretic Foundations

GAN training can be understood through the lens of **game theory**, specifically **two-player zero-sum games**.

**Definition (Zero-Sum Game):**
A game where one player's gain is exactly balanced by the other player's loss:
$$V(G, D) + V(D, G) = 0$$

**Definition (Nash Equilibrium):**
A strategy profile $(G^*, D^*)$ is a Nash equilibrium if neither player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy:
$$V(G^*, D^*) \geq V(G, D^*) \quad \forall G$$
$$V(G^*, D^*) \leq V(G^*, D) \quad \forall D$$

### 2.2 The Minimax Theorem

For zero-sum games, von Neumann's minimax theorem states:

$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \max_D \min_G V(D, G)$$

This equality holds when:
- The strategy spaces are convex
- The value function is convex-concave
- Saddle points exist

**Theorem (GAN Nash Equilibrium):**
For the GAN objective, the unique Nash equilibrium occurs when:
1. $p_g = p_{data}$ (generator perfectly matches data distribution)
2. $D^*(x) = \frac{1}{2}$ for all $x$ (discriminator cannot distinguish)

At this equilibrium, the value function achieves:
$$V(D^*, G^*) = -\log 4 = -2\log 2$$

### 2.3 Proof of Global Optimality

**Proposition:** The global minimum of the virtual training criterion $C(G) = \max_D V(D, G)$ is achieved if and only if $p_g = p_{data}$.

**Proof:**

For fixed $G$, the optimal discriminator is:
$$D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)}$$

Substituting into the objective:
$$C(G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}}\left[\log \frac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g}\left[\log \frac{p_g(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)}\right]$$

Adding and subtracting $\log 2$:
$$C(G) = -\log 4 + KL\left(p_{data} \bigg\| \frac{p_{data} + p_g}{2}\right) + KL\left(p_g \bigg\| \frac{p_{data} + p_g}{2}\right)$$

Recognizing that:
$$KL(P \| Q) + KL(R \| Q) = 2 \cdot JSD(P \| R)$$

where $Q = \frac{P+R}{2}$, we obtain:
$$C(G) = -\log 4 + 2 \cdot JSD(p_{data} \| p_g)$$

Since $JSD \geq 0$ with equality iff $P = R$, the minimum $C^* = -\log 4$ is achieved only when $p_g = p_{data}$. ∎

### 2.4 Practical Implications

| Theoretical Property | Practical Challenge |
|---------------------|---------------------|
| Unique equilibrium | Non-convex optimization landscape |
| Simultaneous updates | Sequential gradient descent |
| Infinite capacity | Finite network capacity |
| Global convergence | Local minima and saddle points |

## 3. Jensen-Shannon Divergence and Optimal Discriminator

### 3.1 f-Divergences Background

**Definition (f-Divergence):**
For convex function $f$ with $f(1) = 0$:
$$D_f(P \| Q) = \int q(x) f\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx$$

Common f-divergences:

| Divergence | $f(u)$ | Formula |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| KL | $u \log u$ | $\int p \log \frac{p}{q}$ |
| Reverse KL | $-\log u$ | $\int q \log \frac{q}{p}$ |
| $\chi^2$ | $(u-1)^2$ | $\int \frac{(p-q)^2}{q}$ |
| JSD | $-(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}{2} + u\log u$ | $\frac{1}{2}KL(P\|M) + \frac{1}{2}KL(Q\|M)$ |

where $M = \frac{P+Q}{2}$

### 3.2 Jensen-Shannon Divergence

**Definition:**
$$JSD(P \| Q) = \frac{1}{2}KL\left(P \bigg\| \frac{P+Q}{2}\right) + \frac{1}{2}KL\left(Q \bigg\| \frac{P+Q}{2}\right)$$

**Properties:**
- Symmetric: $JSD(P \| Q) = JSD(Q \| P)$
- Bounded: $0 \leq JSD(P \| Q) \leq \log 2$
- Zero iff identical: $JSD(P \| Q) = 0 \iff P = Q$
- Metric-like but not a true metric

### 3.3 GANs as Minimizing JSD

**Theorem:** The GAN objective is equivalent to minimizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence between $p_{data}$ and $p_g$.

**Proof:**
Starting from the optimal discriminator:
$$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)}$$

The generator's objective becomes:
$$\min_G \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g}[\log(1 - D^*(x))]$$

Expanding:
$$= \min_G \int p_g(x) \log\left(\frac{p_g(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)}\right) dx$$

Adding the discriminator's term and simplifying:
$$V(D^*, G) = -\log 4 + 2 \cdot JSD(p_{data} \| p_g)$$

Therefore, minimizing $V(D^*, G)$ is equivalent to minimizing $JSD(p_{data} \| p_g)$. ∎

### 3.4 Limitations of JSD

**Problem: Vanishing Gradients**

When the discriminator is too good ($D(x) \approx 1$ for real, $D(x) \approx 0$ for fake):
$$\nabla_\theta \log(1 - D(G(z))) \approx 0$$

This creates a **gradient saturation** problem where the generator receives no learning signal.

**Problem: Disconnected Supports**

If $p_{data}$ and $p_g$ have disjoint support (common in high dimensions), $JSD$ saturates at $\log 2$, providing no gradient information about how to move distributions closer.

## 4. Training Instability and Mode Collapse

### 4.1 Sources of Instability

GAN training faces several fundamental challenges:

**1. Non-Stationary Objective**
- The discriminator's optimal strategy changes as $G$ improves
- Creates a "moving target" problem

**2. Non-Convergence**
- Gradient descent on non-convex games may not converge
- Oscillations and limit cycles are common

**3. Unbalanced Competition**
- If $D$ is too strong: Generator gets no gradient signal
- If $D$ is too weak: Generator receives poor guidance

### 4.2 Mode Collapse: Mechanisms and Causes

**Definition (Mode Collapse):**
A failure mode where the generator learns to produce only a limited subset of the data distribution, ignoring other valid modes.

**Mathematical Characterization:**

Let $p_{data} = \sum_{i=1}^k \pi_i p_i$ be a mixture distribution with $k$ modes.

Mode collapse occurs when $p_g \approx p_j$ for some $j$, ignoring modes $\{p_i\}_{i \neq j}$.

**Causes of Mode Collapse:**

1. **JSD Saturation:**
   - When $p_g$ concentrates on a subset of modes
   - $JSD(p_{data} \| p_g)$ may still be relatively small
   - Insufficient penalty for missing modes

2. **Discriminator Blind Spots:**
   - Discriminator may have regions of high uncertainty
   - Generator exploits these to produce low-quality samples

3. **Gradient Direction:**
   - Generator gradient points toward nearest mode
   - No incentive to explore distant modes

4. **Training Dynamics:**
   ```
   Mode Collapse Cycle:
   1. Generator discovers easy mode → exploits it
   2. Discriminator learns to detect this mode
   3. Generator switches to different mode
   4. Cycle repeats, never covering all modes
   ```

### 4.3 Quantifying Mode Collapse

**Mode Coverage Ratio:**
$$\text{MCR} = \frac{\text{Number of modes captured by } p_g}{\text{Total number of modes in } p_{data}}$$

**Reverse KL Interpretation:**
Mode collapse is related to minimizing reverse KL divergence:
$$KL(p_g \| p_{data}) = \int p_g \log \frac{p_g}{p_{data}}$$

This objective heavily penalizes generating samples where $p_{data} = 0$, encouraging the generator to be conservative and stick to high-probability regions.

### 4.4 Architectural Solutions

| Technique | Mechanism | Effectiveness |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| Minibatch discrimination | Compare samples within batch | Moderate |
| Unrolled GANs | Lookahead gradient computation | High (expensive) |
| Multi-scale structure | Hierarchical generation | High |
| Wasserstein loss | Continuous distance metric | Very High |

## 5. Wasserstein GAN Theory

### 5.1 Motivation: Earth Mover's Distance

The **Wasserstein distance** (Earth Mover's Distance) addresses the limitations of JSD by providing a continuous, meaningful distance metric even for distributions with disjoint support.

**Intuition:**
Imagine $p$ and $q$ as piles of dirt. The Wasserstein distance measures the minimum "work" required to transform one pile into the other.

### 5.2 Mathematical Definition

**Definition (Wasserstein-1 Distance / Earth Mover's Distance):**
$$W_1(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \gamma}[\|x - y\|]$$

where $\Pi(P, Q)$ is the set of all joint distributions with marginals $P$ and $Q$.

**Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality:**
$$W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \leq 1} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}[f(x)]$$

where the supremum is over all **1-Lipschitz** functions $f$.

### 5.3 Wasserstein GAN Formulation

**Theorem (WGAN):** Replacing the discriminator with a critic that approximates a 1-Lipschitz function yields:

$$\min_G \max_{\|D\|_L \leq 1} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}}[D(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[D(G(z))]$$

**Key Advantages:**

1. **Continuous Gradients:** $W_1$ is continuous and differentiable everywhere
2. **Meaningful Distance:** Provides useful gradients even for disjoint supports
3. **Correlates with Quality:** Lower $W_1$ generally means better sample quality

### 5.4 Lipschitz Constraint Enforcement

**Method 1: Weight Clipping**
$$w \leftarrow \text{clip}(w, -c, c)$$
- Simple but limits network capacity
- May create pathological behavior

**Method 2: Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP)**
$$\mathcal{L}_{GP} = \lambda \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}[(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x})\|_2 - 1)^2]$$

where $\hat{x} = \epsilon x + (1-\epsilon)G(z)$ for $\epsilon \sim U[0,1]$.

**Theorem (WGAN-GP):** The gradient penalty encourages the critic to be approximately 1-Lipschitz in the region of interest.

### 5.5 Comparison: JSD vs Wasserstein

| Property | JSD (Original GAN) | Wasserstein (WGAN) |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Continuity | Discontinuous | Continuous |
| Gradients | Vanish with good D | Always informative |
| Training stability | Unstable | More stable |
| Mode coverage | Poor | Better |
| Convergence | Oscillatory | Smoother |
| Computational cost | Lower | Higher |

## 6. Spectral Normalization Theory

### 6.1 Lipschitz Constants and Spectral Norm

**Definition (Spectral Norm):**
For a matrix $W$, the spectral norm is:
$$\sigma(W) = \max_{h \neq 0} \frac{\|Wh\|_2}{\|h\|_2} = \sqrt{\lambda_{max}(W^T W)}$$

where $\lambda_{max}$ denotes the largest eigenvalue.

**Property:** For a linear layer $f(x) = Wx$, the Lipschitz constant equals $\sigma(W)$.

### 6.2 Spectral Normalization for GANs

**Theorem (Spectral Normalization):** Normalizing each layer's weight matrix by its spectral norm ensures the entire network is 1-Lipschitz:

$$\bar{W}_{SN} = \frac{W}{\sigma(W)}$$

**Proof Sketch:**
For a neural network $f = f_n \circ f_{n-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1$:
$$\text{Lip}(f) \leq \prod_{i=1}^n \text{Lip}(f_i)$$

For linear layers with spectral normalization:
$$\text{Lip}(f_i) = \sigma(\bar{W}_{SN}) = 1$$

Therefore, $\text{Lip}(f) \leq 1$. ∎

### 6.3 Power Iteration Method

Computing $\sigma(W)$ exactly via SVD is expensive. The **power iteration method** provides an efficient approximation:

```python
Algorithm: Power Iteration
1. Initialize u randomly
2. For k iterations:
   v ← W^T u / ||W^T u||
   u ← Wv / ||Wv||
3. Return σ ≈ u^T W v
```

**Complexity:** $O(d^2)$ per iteration vs $O(d^3)$ for full SVD.

### 6.4 Comparison with Other Normalization

| Method | Constraint | Computational Cost | Effectiveness |
|--------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| Weight Clipping | $\|W\|_\infty \leq c$ | Low | Poor capacity |
| Gradient Penalty | Soft Lipschitz | High (forward+backward) | Good |
| Spectral Norm | $\sigma(W) = 1$ | Medium (power iter) | Excellent |
| Layer Norm | Mean=0, Var=1 | Low | No Lipschitz guarantee |
| Batch Norm | Per-batch stats | Low | Training instability |

## 7. Progressive Growing Theory

### 7.1 Multi-Scale Generation

Progressive Growing of GANs (PGGAN) introduces a training methodology where both generator and discriminator are built up progressively, starting from low resolution.

**Core Principle:**
Learning coarse-to-fine structure is easier than learning full resolution directly.

### 7.2 Progressive Training Schedule

**Phase Structure:**
```python
Phase 1: 4×4 resolution
  └─ Train G_4 and D_4

Phase 2: 8×8 resolution
  └─ Fade in layers to G_8 and D_8
  └─ Blend: α·G_4 + (1-α)·G_8

Phase 3: 16×16 resolution
  └─ Fade in additional layers
  └─ Continue blending

... up to target resolution (1024×1024)
```

### 7.3 Smooth Transition Mechanism

**Fade-in Layer:**
During transition from $2^n \times 2^n$ to $2^{n+1} \times 2^{n+1}$:

$$G_{new}(z) = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{upsample}(G_{old}(z)) + \alpha \cdot G_{additional}(z)$$

where $\alpha$ linearly increases from 0 to 1 over training iterations.

### 7.4 Theoretical Justification

**Benefit 1: Curriculum Learning**
- Early layers learn coarse structure (pose, general shape)
- Later layers refine details (texture, fine features)
- Natural curriculum emerges from the architecture

**Benefit 2: Stabilized Training**
- Lower resolution = smaller latent space
- Easier to achieve good coverage of data manifold
- Progressive refinement prevents mode collapse

**Benefit 3: Computational Efficiency**
- Early training is fast (small networks)
- Most iterations at lower resolution
- Total training time comparable to fixed architecture

### 7.5 Minibatch Standard Deviation

To encourage diversity, PGGAN adds a feature to the discriminator:

$$\text{MinibatchStd}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$$

This is concatenated as an additional feature channel, forcing the discriminator to consider batch statistics and thereby encouraging the generator to produce diverse samples.

## 8. StyleGAN Architecture Innovations

### 8.1 Beyond Progressive Growing

StyleGAN (Karras et al., 2019) builds on PGGAN but introduces fundamental architectural innovations inspired by style transfer literature.

### 8.2 Style-Based Generator Architecture

**Key Innovation:** Instead of feeding latent code $z$ only at the input, StyleGAN uses **adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN)** to control each layer's style.

**Architecture Components:**

```python
Mapping Network: z (512-dim) → w (512-dim)
  └─ 8-layer MLP
  └─ Disentangles latent space

Synthesis Network: w → image
  └─ Constant learned input (4×4×512)
  └─ For each resolution layer:
      ├─ Upsample
      ├─ Convolution
      ├─ AdaIN(w) ← style injection
      └─ Noise injection
```

### 8.3 Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN)

**Definition:**
$$\text{AdaIN}(x, y) = \sigma(y) \cdot \frac{x - \mu(x)}{\sigma(x)} + \mu(y)$$

where $y$ is the style vector derived from $w$.

**Interpretation:**
- $\mu(x), \sigma(x)$: Normalize content
- $\mu(y), \sigma(y)$: Apply style

### 8.4 Disentangled Latent Space

**Mapping Network Purpose:**
Transform $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ into an **intermediate latent space** $W$ that is more disentangled.

**Properties of W Space:**
- Better separation of factors of variation
- Linear subspaces correspond to semantic attributes
- More suitable for style mixing and controlled generation

### 8.5 Stochastic Variation

**Noise Injection:**
Uncorrelated Gaussian noise is added after each convolution:

$$x' = x + \text{noise} \cdot \gamma$$

where $\gamma$ is learned per-channel.

**Effect:**
- Adds stochastic detail (hair, pores, stubble)
- Does not affect overall structure
- Creates natural-looking variation

### 8.6 Style Mixing

**Technique:** Use different $w$ vectors for different layers:

```python
# Style mixing example
w1 = mapping_network(z1)  # Coarse styles
w2 = mapping_network(z2)  # Fine styles

# Apply w1 to coarse layers (4×4 - 8×8)
# Apply w2 to fine layers (16×16 - 1024×1024)
```

**Semantic Layer Groups:**
| Resolution | Controls |
|-----------|----------|
| 4×4 - 8×8 | Pose, general shape, glasses |
| 16×16 - 32×32 | Facial features, hair style |
| 64×64 - 1024×1024 | Color scheme, fine details |

### 8.7 StyleGAN2 and StyleGAN3 Improvements

**StyleGAN2 Fixes:**
- Removed progressive growing artifacts
- Revised normalization (weight demodulation)
- Improved path length regularization

**StyleGAN3 Improvements:**
- Translation and rotation equivariance
- Better handling of texture sticking
- Continuous motion in latent space

## 9. Conditional Generation Techniques

### 9.1 Conditional GAN Framework

**Objective:** Generate samples conditioned on auxiliary information $y$:
$$x \sim p_g(x | y)$$

**Conditional GAN Objective:**
$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\log D(x, y)] + \mathbb{E}_{z,y}[\log(1 - D(G(z, y), y))]$$

### 9.2 Conditioning Mechanisms

**Method 1: Concatenation**
- Concatenate $y$ with $z$ at input
- Concatenate $y$ with image at discriminator

**Method 2: Projection Discriminator**
$$D(x, y) = \phi(x)^T \psi(y)$$
- Encourages feature alignment
- More stable training

**Method 3: Auxiliary Classifier (AC-GAN)**
$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{GAN} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{classifier}$$
- Discriminator also predicts class label
- Strong conditioning signal

### 9.3 cGAN with Projection

**Miyato & Koyama (2018):**
$$D(x, y) = \frac{\phi(x)^T \psi(y)}{\|\phi(x)\| \|\psi(y)\|}$$

**Advantages:**
- No additional parameters for conditioning
- Works with any number of classes
- More stable than concatenation

### 9.4 Class-Conditional Batch Normalization

**Technique:** Use separate batch norm parameters per class:

$$\text{CCBN}(x, y) = \gamma_y \cdot \frac{x - \mu_y}{\sqrt{\sigma_y^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta_y$$

**Application:**
- BigGAN uses this for large-scale conditional generation
- Each class learns its own normalization statistics

### 9.5 Hierarchical Conditioning

For complex conditional generation (e.g., text-to-image):

**Stacked Architecture:**
```python
Stage 1: Text → Low-res image (64×64)
Stage 2: Low-res + Text → Mid-res (128×128)
Stage 3: Mid-res + Text → High-res (256×256)
```

**Attention Mechanisms:**
- Word-level attention to image regions
- Fine-grained control over generation

## 10. GAN Evaluation Theory

### 10.1 Challenges in GAN Evaluation

Unlike supervised learning with clear metrics (accuracy, loss), GAN evaluation faces:

1. **No explicit likelihood** (for most GAN variants)
2. **Multiple objectives** (quality vs. diversity)
3. **Mode coverage** vs. sample quality trade-off
4. **Human perception** is the ultimate judge

### 10.2 Inception Score (IS)

**Definition:**
$$IS(G) = \exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g} KL(p(y|x) \| p(y))\right)$$

where:
- $p(y|x)$: Conditional label distribution (from Inception network)
- $p(y) = \int p(y|x) p_g(x) dx$: Marginal label distribution

**Interpretation:**
- High $p(y|x)$: Image has clear, distinct class (high quality)
- High entropy $p(y)$: Diverse class distribution (good coverage)
- IS balances both objectives

**Properties:**
| Aspect | Evaluation |
|--------|-----------|
| Quality sensitivity | High |
| Mode coverage | Moderate |
| Sensitivity to class imbalance | High |
| Computational cost | Low |

**Limitations:**
- Favors "memorization" of training data
- Insensitive to intra-class diversity
- Depends on Inception network's classification ability

### 10.3 Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)

**Definition:**
$$FID = \|\mu_{real} - \mu_{gen}\|^2 + \text{Tr}\left(\Sigma_{real} + \Sigma_{gen} - 2\sqrt{\Sigma_{real}\Sigma_{gen}}\right)$$

where $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are mean and covariance of Inception features.

**Interpretation:**
- Measures distance between feature distributions
- Lower FID = more similar to real data
- Captures both quality and diversity

**Advantages over IS:**
- More robust to noise
- Better correlation with human judgment
- Measures actual distribution distance

**Properties:**
| Aspect | Evaluation |
|--------|-----------|
| Quality sensitivity | High |
| Mode coverage | High |
| Sample size sensitivity | Moderate |
| Computational cost | Medium |

### 10.4 Precision and Recall

**Motivation:** Separate quality (precision) from diversity (recall).

**Precision:** Fraction of generated samples that are "realistic"
$$\text{Precision} = \frac{|\{x_g : x_g \text{ is realistic}\}|}{|\{x_g\}|}$$

**Recall:** Fraction of real data manifold covered by generated samples
$$\text{Recall} = \frac{|\{x_r : x_r \text{ is matched by } x_g\}|}{|\{x_r\}|}$$

**Implementation:**
1. Extract features using Inception/VGG
2. Define "realistic" via k-NN in feature space
3. Compute precision/recall based on manifold coverage

**Trade-off Analysis:**
```python
High Precision, Low Recall  → Good quality, poor diversity (mode collapse)
Low Precision, High Recall  → Poor quality, good diversity
High Precision, High Recall → Ideal GAN
```

### 10.5 Kernel Inception Distance (KID)

**Definition:**
$$KID = \mathbb{E}_{x,x' \sim p_{data}}[k(x, x')] + \mathbb{E}_{y,y' \sim p_g}[k(y, y')] - 2\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}, y \sim p_g}[k(x, y)]$$

where $k$ is a polynomial kernel.

**Advantages:**
- Unbiased estimator (unlike FID)
- Works better with small sample sizes
- No assumptions about Gaussianity

### 10.6 Comparison of Metrics

| Metric | What it Measures | Best For | Limitations |
|--------|-----------------|----------|-------------|
| IS | Quality + Diversity | Quick evaluation | Class bias |
| FID | Distribution distance | Standard benchmark | Sample size dependent |
| Precision | Quality | Quality-focused analysis | Ignores diversity |
| Recall | Diversity | Mode coverage analysis | Ignores quality |
| KID | Distribution distance | Small sample evaluation | Less interpretable |
| LPIPS | Perceptual similarity | Image similarity | Pairwise only |

## 11. Introduction to Diffusion Models

### 11.1 Limitations of GANs

Despite their success, GANs have fundamental limitations:

1. **Training instability:** Requires careful balancing of G and D
2. **Mode collapse:** Difficulty covering full data distribution
3. **No explicit likelihood:** Cannot compute $p(x)$
4. **Non-reversible:** Cannot easily invert the generation process

### 11.2 Diffusion Models: Core Concept

Diffusion models (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, DDPM) take a fundamentally different approach:

**Forward Process (Diffusion):**
Gradually add Gaussian noise to data over $T$ steps:
$$q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)$$

**Reverse Process (Denoising):**
Learn to reverse the diffusion:
$$p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t, t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t))$$

### 11.3 Forward Process Properties

**Closed-form Sampling:**
$$q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) I)$$

where $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$.

**Key Property:** Can sample $x_t$ directly from $x_0$ without iterating through intermediate steps.

### 11.4 Training Objective

**Simplified Loss:**
$$\mathcal{L}_{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, t, \epsilon}\left[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\right]$$

where:
- $x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon$
- $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$
- $\epsilon_\theta$ is the noise prediction network

**Interpretation:** Learn to predict the noise that was added.

### 11.5 Comparison: GANs vs Diffusion Models

| Aspect | GANs | Diffusion Models |
|--------|------|-----------------|
| Training stability | Unstable | Stable |
| Mode coverage | Prone to collapse | Excellent |
| Sample quality | Very high | Very high |
| Generation speed | Fast (single forward) | Slow (many steps) |
| Likelihood | Implicit | Explicit |
| Invertibility | No | Yes |
| Conditioning | Moderate | Excellent |
| Training time | Fast | Slow |

### 11.6 Accelerated Sampling

**Problem:** Original DDPM requires 1000+ steps for generation.

**Solutions:**

| Method | Approach | Speedup |
|--------|----------|---------|
| DDIM | Non-Markovian sampling | 10-50× |
| Progressive distillation | Student-teacher | 1000× |
| Latent diffusion | Operate in latent space | 10× |
| Consistency models | Single-step generation | 1000× |

### 11.7 Connection to Score-Based Models

**Score Function:**
$$s_\theta(x, t) = \nabla_x \log p_\theta(x, t)$$

**Equivalence:**
The diffusion model's noise prediction is related to the score:
$$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \propto -\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot s_\theta(x_t, t)$$

This connects diffusion models to **score matching** and **Langevin dynamics**.

## Common Misconceptions

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: GANs always produce better images than diffusion models">
While GANs were the quality leaders through ~2020, modern diffusion models (DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion, Imagen) match or exceed GAN quality while offering stable training, explicit likelihood, and better mode coverage. GANs retain the advantage in **inference speed** (single forward pass vs. hundreds of denoising steps), but "GAN = best quality" is no longer universally true.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: Mode collapse means the GAN generates identical images">
Mode collapse does NOT mean every output is pixel-identical. It means the generator covers only a **subset of modes** (clusters) in the data distribution. A mode-collapsed GAN on CIFAR-10 might generate diverse-looking cars and planes but never produce cats or ships. Intra-mode variation can still be high — it's the **missing modes** that define collapse, not output uniformity.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: WGAN completely solves training instability">
WGAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) significantly improves training stability, but it does **not** eliminate all failure modes. WGAN-GP still suffers from: (1) sensitivity to the gradient penalty coefficient λ, (2) computational overhead of computing per-sample gradients for the penalty, (3) the penalty is only enforced along interpolation lines — not globally. Spectral normalization (SN-GAN) offers a cheaper alternative that works well in practice.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Misconception: FID score is an absolute quality measure">
FID depends on: (1) the specific Inception v3 weights used, (2) the sample size (fewer samples → higher variance), (3) the reference dataset statistics. An FID of 10 on FFHQ is NOT comparable to FID 10 on CIFAR-10 — the datasets have different feature distributions. Always compare FID scores computed with the **same reference set, same sample size, and same Inception checkpoint**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
- **GANs learn via a minimax game** where the discriminator provides an adaptive loss function — understanding the game-theoretic formulation (JSD minimization, Nash equilibrium) is essential for diagnosing training failures.
- **Wasserstein distance + Lipschitz constraints** (via gradient penalty or spectral normalization) are the most important practical improvements over the original GAN — they provide meaningful gradients even when distributions don't overlap.
- **StyleGAN's mapping network + AdaIN** revolutionized controllable generation by disentangling the latent space — separating coarse structure from fine detail enables style mixing and semantic editing.
- **Always evaluate with multiple metrics** — FID for overall distribution similarity, Precision/Recall for quality vs. diversity decomposition, and visual inspection for artifacts. No single number captures generation quality.
</Callout>

## Key Insights Summary

### Theoretical Foundations
1. **GANs minimize JSD** between data and generated distributions
2. **Nash equilibrium** occurs when $p_g = p_{data}$ and $D(x) = 0.5$
3. **Wasserstein distance** provides continuous gradients unlike JSD

### Training Challenges
1. **Mode collapse** arises from JSD properties and gradient dynamics
2. **Instability** comes from non-stationary objectives and non-convex games
3. **Lipschitz constraints** are essential for WGAN stability

### Architectural Innovations
1. **Progressive growing** enables stable high-resolution generation
2. **StyleGAN** separates content and style through AdaIN
3. **Spectral normalization** provides elegant Lipschitz constraint

### Evaluation
1. **FID** is the standard metric for overall quality
2. **Precision/Recall** separate quality from diversity
3. **Multiple metrics** should be used for comprehensive evaluation

### Future Directions
1. **Diffusion models** offer stable training with explicit likelihood
2. **Hybrid approaches** combine GAN speed with diffusion quality
3. **Conditional generation** enables controllable, application-specific models

## Common Pitfalls and Solutions

| Pitfall | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Vanishing gradients | Too strong discriminator | Use WGAN or adjust learning rates |
| Mode collapse | JSD limitations | Minibatch discrimination, WGAN |
| Training oscillation | Non-convergence | Reduce learning rate, use spectral norm |
| Checkerboard artifacts | Transposed convolutions | Use resize-convolution |
| Blurry images | L2 loss in pixel space | Use perceptual loss |
| Poor conditioning | Weak conditional signal | Use projection discriminator |

## References

1. Goodfellow, I., et al. (2014). Generative Adversarial Networks. NeurIPS. — The foundational paper introducing the adversarial framework, minimax objective, and proof that the global optimum corresponds to $p_g = p_{data}$ via JSD minimization.
2. Arjovsky, M., et al. (2017). Wasserstein GAN. ICML. — Replaced JSD with Earth Mover's distance via Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, providing continuous gradients even for disjoint supports; introduced weight clipping for Lipschitz enforcement.
3. Gulrajani, I., et al. (2017). Improved Training of Wasserstein GANs. NeurIPS. — Proposed gradient penalty on interpolated samples as a superior alternative to weight clipping for enforcing the 1-Lipschitz constraint; became the standard WGAN training recipe.
4. Miyato, T., et al. (2018). Spectral Normalization for GANs. ICLR. — Introduced spectral normalization via power iteration to enforce 1-Lipschitz discriminators cheaply, avoiding both weight clipping's capacity issues and GP's computational overhead.
5. Karras, T., et al. (2018). Progressive Growing of GANs. ICLR. — Pioneered coarse-to-fine training by progressively adding layers, enabling stable generation at 1024×1024; introduced minibatch standard deviation for diversity.
6. Karras, T., et al. (2019). A Style-Based Generator Architecture for GANs. CVPR. — Introduced the mapping network + AdaIN style injection paradigm, disentangling coarse and fine attributes; enabled style mixing and high-fidelity face synthesis.
7. Brock, A., et al. (2019). Large Scale GAN Training for High Fidelity Natural Image Synthesis (BigGAN). ICLR. — Scaled conditional GAN training to ImageNet with class-conditional batch normalization, orthogonal regularization, and the truncation trick for quality-diversity control.
8. Heusel, M., et al. (2017). GANs Trained by a Two Time-Scale Update Rule Converge to a Local Nash Equilibrium. NeurIPS. — Introduced Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) as the standard GAN evaluation metric based on feature-level distribution comparison.
9. Salimans, T., et al. (2016). Improved Techniques for Training GANs. NeurIPS. — Proposed feature matching, minibatch discrimination, historical averaging, one-sided label smoothing, and the Inception Score (IS) metric for GAN evaluation.
10. Ho, J., et al. (2020). Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models. NeurIPS. — Demonstrated that iterative denoising with a simple MSE loss achieves image quality competitive with GANs while offering stable training and full mode coverage.
11. Mirza, M., & Osindero, S. (2014). Conditional Generative Adversarial Nets. arXiv:1411.1784. — First formalization of conditioning GANs on auxiliary information (class labels, attributes), enabling controlled generation by feeding $y$ to both generator and discriminator.
12. Radford, A., Metz, L., & Chintala, S. (2016). Unsupervised Representation Learning with Deep Convolutional GANs (DCGAN). ICLR. — Established architectural guidelines for stable convolutional GANs: strided convolutions, batch normalization, ReLU/LeakyReLU activations, and latent space arithmetic.
13. Karras, T., et al. (2020). Analyzing and Improving the Image Quality of StyleGAN (StyleGAN2). CVPR. — Removed progressive growing artifacts via skip connections, replaced AdaIN with weight demodulation, and introduced path length regularization for smoother latent space.
